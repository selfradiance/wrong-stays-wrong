# The Body Was the Test

For months, I had an electrical zap in my right foot. It appeared around the second toe during a particular kind of push-off. I could walk normally most of the time. I could twist, squeeze, bend, and pull the toes without reproducing it. Then one ordinary movement would catch the right angle and the nerve would fire.

It was not disabling. It was more like a splinter I could not remove. I wanted to run again, and every sudden jolt reminded me that I did not understand what was happening.

I took the problem to AI. That was not one conversation followed by one answer. It unfolded over months. There were changing hypotheses about neuropathy, the second metatarsophalangeal joint, the plantar plate, a tendon, a stress injury, and a small sensory nerve. Some clues turned out not to be clues at all. At one point ChatGPT treated tight shoes as a trigger when they were merely something I disliked. I corrected it, and the case changed.

I could have gone to a podiatrist. Perhaps I still will if the condition changes or fails to settle. But I did not want a label unless the label changed what I could do. I was less interested in naming the structure than in discovering the cause of the electrical sensation and finding a way to stop provoking it.

My father was a doctor and a good diagnostician. I also saw how any doctor’s judgment is bounded by a particular knowledge base and way of looking. Doctors must make guesses too. A scan can find something without establishing that the finding caused the symptom. Treatment can become focused on suppressing the symptom without discovering the mechanical situation producing it.

AI is not free of those limitations. It guesses as well. The difference in this case was that I could question several systems, carry their disagreements between them, correct their assumptions, and continue the investigation for as long as I found it useful.

I asked ChatGPT to prepare a detailed account that I could take to Claude, Grok, and Gemini. The more viewpoints, the merrier. Most of the useful exchange ultimately ran between ChatGPT and Claude, with me carrying the information back and forth.

We tried home tests. I ground the joint, resisted extension, manipulated the toes, compressed the foot, and performed calf raises. Almost everything was silent. That weakened several of the earlier theories. Claude began to favor an irritable sensory nerve that fired only when a particular mechanical threshold was crossed. ChatGPT agreed with the direction while resisting Claude’s certainty about the exact anatomical structure.

The important change came when I described the sensation more precisely. During a loaded calf raise on hardwood, it radiated toward the tip of the toe. It did not travel back into the foot. It occurred while the toe was partly bent, around the position it reaches during push-off.

Now we had something we had lacked for months: a reproducible trigger.

Claude suggested repeating the same movement in the stiffest shoe I owned. I put on a cycling shoe. Its sole barely bends. On the same hardwood floor, performing the same movement, I could not make the nerve fire.

Flexible forefoot, electrical zap. Rigid forefoot, nothing.

That was not an anatomical diagnosis. It was more useful than one. We had isolated an operative mechanism: loaded bending through the forefoot was involved, and preventing that bending prevented the symptom.

The models then helped identify running shoes with stiff or rockered soles. Instead of forcing the toes to bend through push-off, these shoes encourage the entire foot to roll forward. I tried a Brooks Ghost Max.

Walking produced no zap. Jogging produced no zap. Faster intervals produced no zap. I sprinted. Nothing. I came home and performed full-effort vertical jumps. Still nothing.

My initial enthusiasm was enormous. It remains to be tested.

I ordered the Ghost Max in a wider size and a Hoka Bondi as a second version of the same mechanical idea. I also ordered Hoka recovery slides for casual walking. The immediate objective is simple: stop unnecessarily firing the nerve. Give it time without repeatedly recreating the condition that irritates it.

That may be management rather than cure. The shoe may simply carry me around the troublesome movement forever. The more interesting possibility is that, after weeks or months without aggravation, the sensitivity itself will fade. On occasion I can repeat the controlled bending test on a hard floor. If the movement that once reliably produced the zap eventually becomes completely uneventful in an ordinary flexible shoe, that will be much stronger evidence that the condition has settled rather than merely been bypassed.

I did not notice a single injury that started this. The sensation seemed to arise by itself. Perhaps it can disappear in much the same way. I am not going to force it to disappear. I allow the electrical irritability to settle and disappear. Then we will see what the body actually does.

This is not a story about asking an AI a medical question and receiving the answer. It is not a panacea or an argument that people should avoid doctors. The investigation took months and many conversations. The models made mistakes, inherited bad assumptions, became too certain, and changed their minds.

What accelerated the process was collaboration. One system proposed a possibility. Another challenged it. I supplied missing history. My body supplied experimental results. I decided which claims deserved to be tested and carried what we learned into the next conversation.

The intelligence was not located in any single participant. It was in the loop.

I have used that loop in coding: several AIs examining a problem from different angles while I direct the work and judge the results. This time I applied it to my body. The eventual breakthrough did not arrive as a diagnosis. It arrived as a comparison simple enough to perform on my hardwood floor.

The body was the test.

**Claim 1 — Horizon: 2026-12-07.** By that date, I will complete three separate hard-surface runs of at least twenty minutes in stiff or rockered footwear without an electrical zap during the run, lingering symptoms afterward, or worsening the following morning. I will also complete, on three separate days, ten controlled single-leg calf raises on a hard floor in ordinary flexible footwear without a zap or lingering symptoms. Every qualifying attempt will be recorded, including unsuccessful attempts. If the running condition succeeds but the flexible-footwear test does not, the claim is wrong: the mechanical workaround held, but the underlying sensitivity did not disappear by the horizon.

**Claim 2 — Horizon: 2028-09-07.** Between this chapter’s sealing and that date, I will document at least one additional non-emergency health or physical problem for which an iterative collaboration involving at least two general-purpose AI systems produces a specific action that I follow for at least thirty days. The record must preserve the initial problem, material disagreements or changing hypotheses, the action chosen, and its outcome. It will count only if the action is subsequently supported either by sustained measurable improvement over those thirty days or by an independent qualified clinician’s assessment. Merely asking several systems the same question, receiving matching answers, or feeling temporarily reassured will not count.

The first claim tests whether today’s excitement survives contact with time. The second tests whether this method can produce another result rather than becoming a heroic story built around one fortunate case.

The models helped formulate both claims. I agreed to them. Neither claim will establish that AI can diagnose illness reliably or that professional medicine is unnecessary. The first can show only what happened with this foot. The second can show only whether I was able to reproduce the collaborative process once.

For now, I know less than the word diagnosis would imply and more than I knew before.

I do not know the exact name of the thing.

I know how to make it stop.
