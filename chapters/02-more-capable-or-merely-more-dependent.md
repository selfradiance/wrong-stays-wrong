# Is AI Making Me More Capable, or Merely More Dependent?

I began with the easy answer: more capable. The qualification arrived almost immediately.

I cannot code. AI has not taught me enough syntax or software architecture to sit down and build anything substantial by myself. I often do not understand half of what the code is doing. Yet I can now cause working software to exist. Before AI, my ability in that world amounted to stick figures. Now I can direct the making of a reasonably good picture without knowing how to mix the paint.

That is not the same as becoming a programmer. It is still a real increase in capability. I can enter a world that was previously closed to me, form an intention inside it, recognize whether the result resembles what I wanted, catch some mistakes, reverse course, and finish a project. If I wanted to learn the underlying craft, AI could help me do that too. I have not done so because coding itself does not interest me enough. The finished artifact and the process of bringing it into existence do.

Music makes the distinction clearer. AI has introduced me to forms I barely knew existed, including shoegaze, and has allowed me to combine styles I would never have thought to place together. A recent album began when an instrumental track suggested a mixture of reggae and something resembling Blind Melon. I added a title and an intention: lyrics with a Vedantic flavor that turned attention inward without preaching. AI supplied most of the lyrics and structure. Then I listened.

My criterion for a good song is often physical. Does my body start moving? If I am dancing, something has connected before I can explain why. AI can generate the candidate, but it cannot currently occupy my body at the moment of recognition. It also cannot rely on a fixed profile of my taste, because my taste changes through the process. I did not know that I wanted this particular mixture until I heard it.

Authorship does not matter much to me. I do not need to claim that every word or sound originated in my head. Participation matters. I need to hear the music, examine the lyrics, reject what does not fit, and remain available for the unexpected turn. If an agent learned my history, generated the songs, published them, and ran the entire operation without me, I would not want it. The output might be competent, but the activity I enjoy would have disappeared.

This is where AI slop enters. Slop is not merely work made with AI. It is work that passed through no sustained attention. Nobody cared enough to listen, question, revise, or be surprised. Human beings can make slop manually. AI simply allows unattended output to multiply.

I do not automate my life. I once automated a YouTube Shorts channel because making the shorts felt useless. Automation made it feel even more useless, so I eliminated the channel. That taught me a better rule. If an activity is enjoyable, I participate in it. If an unenjoyable task is necessary to support something I value, I streamline it. If it is neither enjoyable nor necessary, I stop doing it. Automating an empty activity merely keeps its numbers moving.

There is nevertheless a genuine dependence inside my increased capability. If AI disappeared for thirty days, most of my software work and much of my current music production would stop. But I would not feel that part of my mind had been removed. I would read, play guitar, ride my mountain bike, spend time outside, or do something else. AI has not replaced a craft I once possessed. It has given me access to capabilities I otherwise would not have pursued.

I am also not attached to one provider. I use different systems for different purposes and move when another arrangement works better. I depend upon the availability of the general tool for some activities, but I am not particularly captive to one company. More capable and more dependent are therefore not opposites. Nearly every extension creates some dependence on the thing providing it. The relevant question is whether that dependence enlarges life or begins to narrow it.

I can see one possible narrowing already. My tolerance for ordinary informational pacing has declined. I speed up spoken audio until the faster cadence sounds normal. I rarely have any interest in watching a two-hour video when AI can extract what appears to be five minutes of useful information. I sometimes feel the pull to check YouTube statistics again. The phone, social media, recommendation systems, and analytics all contributed to this environment before generative AI arrived, so I cannot isolate the cause. Still, cognitive life appears to be accelerating, and AI is part of the acceleration.

Refusing to sit through padded media may be discernment rather than damage. The more important test is whether I can still remain with something whose slowness is part of its value. I can sit with horses, notice a leaf, listen closely to a song, read, talk with someone I care about, or close the laptop on a beautiful day. For now, my attention seems to have divided into two speeds: machine speed for information and production, and living speed for experiences that should not be compressed. The danger would be allowing machine speed to colonize everything else.

AI has also changed how I think. I can jamble through an idea and receive a counterargument immediately. A model sometimes exposes an assumption I did not notice or supplies context missing from a claim I encountered online. I do not find being corrected threatening. If I was wrong, I was wrong. That is the premise of this project.

But the model's correction is not proof merely because it sounds informed. AI can challenge a misleading clip and can also produce a persuasive error of its own. The benefit comes from reconsideration plus verification, not from exchanging one unquestioned authority for another. Repeated contact with counterarguments may strengthen my capacity to catch myself, or it may teach me to outsource self-questioning. That remains open.

My circumstances matter. I am retired. I do not have employees, customers, a LinkedIn strategy, or children depending on my next paycheck. I can close the laptop the moment an activity stops being enjoyable. Someone whose employer measures AI-enhanced output every week is conducting a different experiment. I cannot speak for that person, and this chapter should not pretend that I can.

At the deepest level, AI cannot touch what I know without words. Nothing can. At the ordinary human level, however, it has materially changed what I can make, which subjects I can enter, and how easily I can examine my own thinking. It removes grind while leaving me inside the parts of the process I value.

So far, AI is making me more capable, with dependencies attached. It is not merely making me dependent. I can build more without imagining that I personally contain every skill involved. I can be challenged without treating correction as defeat. I can use the machine at machine speed and still walk away from it.

That is the answer today. The purpose of recording it is not to make the answer permanent. It is to leave something specific enough that a later version of me cannot quietly pretend the balance never changed.
